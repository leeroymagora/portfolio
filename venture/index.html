Clean cooking, from biogas to hydrogen.

Leeroy Takudzwa Magora
Founder & CEO, H2ENERGIES · Rusape, Zimbabwe

Many rural households around Rusape cook on firewood over an open fire. H2ENERGIES started as a student project to change that. It is now a registered company working on two fronts: a briquette-and-stove business that can sell today, and the Hydrostove, a solar-powered cookstove that makes its own hydrogen from water.

02 · Product development

Hydrostove 1.0

The Hydrostove makes hydrogen from water using solar power and burns it straight away, so a household never stores a tank of gas. Charlton Mandaza and I designed, built and tested the first unit as our final-year capstone at Ashesi University (grade A).

Design basis and results

Daily hydrogen demand0.09 kg (family of four)
ElectrolyserPEM, 75% efficiency, 4.44 kWh/day
Solar PVAbout 1.24 kW
Hydrogen storageNone: made and burned on demand
Burner orifice1.182 mm
Peak flame temperature (CFD)About 2,170 K
Thermal efficiency (measured)74.3%
Boiling test1 L of water in 10 minutes
Structural testPassed, no damage

03 · Customers and community

Who we build for, and how we earn

What we tested

  • Pilot, Apr–Sep 2025. Under the AI for Health Co-Creation Fund we deployed stoves to 10 households to cut indoor smoke, and ran an awareness campaign that reached 100+ people. Pilot photos
  • Training. Co-design workshops in rural Zimbabwe, and 100+ women trained to cook safely with clean fuels.
  • Why it matters locally. Our National Geographic mapping showed how far the forest around Tsanzaguru has retreated since 2000C7.

What we changed

Hydrogen is the long-term product, but families need cleaner cooking now. In 2026 we moved to a briquettes-first model: briquettes bound with masekesa, a local plant fibre, sold with an efficient stove (46% efficient against the open-fire baseline), while Hydrostove R&D continues in parallel.

The Gushure Community Kitchen

A women-led, earn-to-own hub on our site in Gushure Village. Members use the kitchen’s stoves to make and sell local snacks such as maputi and roasted groundnuts. Part of what they earn goes towards owning their own stove, so the kitchen pays for itself and spreads the stoves into homes.
